# Literature survey: Can the optimization comparison be repeated in a preregistered, informative regime?

Genetic-algorithm comparisons are sensitive to algorithm structure, control parameters, and computational budget. Jenkins et al. compare generational, steady-state, steady-generational, and \((\mu+\mu)\) genetic algorithms on the Schaffer F6 function, using the number of function evaluations as the central performance measure [1]. Their study illustrates why evaluation count is preferable to generation count when update schedules differ: a generational GA evaluates an entire population per cycle, whereas steady-state variants evaluate only one or two offspring. They use a population size of 16, mutation rate of 0.012, a target of fewer than 4,000 evaluations, and 30 runs [1]. However, these settings are described as “optimal,” and the best crossover representatives are selected after comparisons involving observed evaluation performance and significance tests. Thus, the work supports evaluation-budget normalization and repeated stochastic runs, but it does not establish a preregistered comparison in which parameter selection is insulated from the confirmatory data.

The proposed replication should therefore separate calibration from testing. This is particularly important when success is binary—whether a run reaches a predefined target within the evaluation budget. If the calibrated serial algorithm almost never succeeds, a batch-size-16 method has little room to show a detectable disadvantage; if serial success is nearly certain, there is little room to show an advantage. General evolutionary-algorithm theory includes “almost surely” convergence results [3], but such asymptotic guarantees do not identify a finite-budget regime that is statistically informative. Population size, mutation rate, and budget should instead be chosen on separate calibration runs to place serial success away from both zero and one. The success threshold, initialization distribution, stopping rule, and treatment of evaluations within a batch must also be fixed, because otherwise batch execution can receive an unintended budget or stopping advantage.

Parallel-evolutionary work shows that batching and parallelism are practically important, but the retrieved evidence mainly concerns acceleration rather than controlled success comparisons. GAPA supports distributed acceleration of GA-based graph optimization and reports experiments across multiple tasks, datasets, and algorithms [2]. EOS likewise uses a synchronous island model to handle multiple populations in parallel and reports benefits on difficult constrained optimization problems [4]. More generally, hardware comparisons can reverse depending on problem dimensions: Liu et al. find strong GPU acceleration in some regimes but better relative CPU performance when velocity-space grids are large [8]. These studies indicate that wall-clock performance depends on implementation, hardware, and workload. For the proposed batch-size-16 comparison, the primary scientific endpoint should therefore be success under an equal, precisely defined evaluation budget; elapsed time can be secondary and reported with hardware and implementation details.

Preregistration should lock the confirmatory analysis before the batch comparison. Lei, Gelman, and Ghitza distinguish preliminary, non-preregistered investigation from replications conducted on previously unseen datasets under preregistered plans [5]. Applied here, calibration results may inform the operating regime, but calibration seeds or instances must not reappear in the confirmatory sample. Because serial and batch algorithms can be run on the same instance and initialization seed, a paired design can control shared run difficulty. Paired-comparison design is an established experimental framework [6], although the retrieved source addresses block-design construction rather than this specific binary-success analysis. The preregistration should state the paired success contrast, its confidence interval, the number of pairs, and a decision rule based on the interval relative to a prespecified null or practically meaningful margin—not merely whether a \(p\)-value crosses a threshold.

## Gap

None of the provided sources tests whether a serial GA and a batch-size-16 variant differ in finite-budget success after independently calibrating population size, mutation rate, and evaluation budget to produce measurable but nonsaturated serial performance. The open question is whether the earlier optimization comparison persists when tuning is separated from confirmation and the primary paired success effect, confidence interval, sample size, evaluation accounting, and decision rule are preregistered. A new experiment should answer this question while keeping objective, target, initialization, operators, and total evaluation opportunity identical across paired runs.
