# Literature survey: Does the paired calibration effect reach or exceed 0.08 when selection is performed over a substantially larger locked parameter grid while retaining 16 calibration seeds per split?

The retrieved literature provides indirect but useful guidance for this question; none of the sources directly studies a paired calibration effect, the 0.08 threshold, or selection with exactly 16 calibration seeds per split. The closest methodological evidence concerns replication and paired evaluation under stochastic training or resampling. Peng’s 100-seed study shows that conclusions based on very few seeds can be unstable: several apparent directional effects under three seeds disappeared or reversed under 100-seed replication [1]. That study used paired *t* tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and variance comparisons because both methods were evaluated under matched random conditions [1]. For the proposed experiment, the analogous design is to compare calibration outcomes within the same split and seed wherever possible. The primary estimand should be the paired difference in a prespecified calibration metric, with uncertainty across splits and seeds. Because the scientific question concerns whether the effect reaches 0.08—not merely whether it differs from zero—reporting the effect estimate and its uncertainty relative to 0.08 is more informative than a significance test alone.

Selection over a larger parameter grid is a hyperparameter-optimization problem, and the cited optimization literature consistently treats evaluation cost and noisy or inexact objective values as central concerns. Ehrhardt and Roberts explicitly develop tuning methods that permit inexact evaluations of the underlying learning problem rather than assuming exact objective values [3]. Dhar, Kurup, and Shah likewise address unstable convergence in bi-level hyperparameter optimization through regularization [4], while the broader survey by Chen and colleagues formalizes hyperparameter tuning as an outer optimization problem whose evaluations depend on an inner learning problem [5]. Bayesian optimization is presented as an alternative for expensive black-box objectives, balancing exploration and exploitation under limited evaluation budgets [7]. These works do not establish whether enlarging a grid will increase the calibration effect, but they clarify that the selected configuration depends jointly on the search space, evaluation noise, and optimization protocol.

The requirement that the larger grid be locked is therefore methodologically important. A fixed, prespecified grid makes the follow-up a controlled comparison of search-space size rather than an adaptive search altered after observing outcomes. Evaluation and selection should also remain separated: Fong and Holmes characterize cross-validation as an out-of-sample model-comparison mechanism and emphasize that model scores depend on the scoring rule and validation construction [2]. Thus, the experiment should specify whether the 16 calibration seeds are used to select configurations, estimate the final calibration effect, or both. If the same stochastic evaluations serve both purposes, the reported value describes the performance of the complete selection procedure rather than an independently validated effect.

Retaining 16 seeds while substantially increasing the number of candidate configurations leaves a key reliability issue unresolved. Peng’s results do not imply that 16 seeds are inadequate in this setting, but they do demonstrate that low-replication directional patterns can be misleading and that stochastic variability may affect means and variances differently [1]. The experiment should therefore preserve paired seeds across configurations, record the full distribution of paired differences, and distinguish variability across seeds from variability across data splits. It should also report how often the selected configuration changes across splits or seed subsets, since a larger grid may alter selection even when average calibration differences remain small.

## Gap

No retrieved source tests whether a paired calibration effect reaches 0.08 after expanding a locked parameter grid while holding calibration replication at 16 seeds per split. The open question is empirical: does broader selection uncover a reproducible effect of at least 0.08, or does selection variability under the fixed 16-seed budget dominate the apparent gain? A new experiment must prespecify the grid, calibration metric, pairing scheme, selection rule, and threshold analysis, then quantify both the paired effect relative to 0.08 and the stability of the selected configuration.
