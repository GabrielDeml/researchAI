# Literature survey: How do adaptive substitutions behave in executable tests?

Provenance systems generally aim to establish source identity, transformation history, and artifact integrity through signed manifests or non-repudiable records. AMP, for example, associates media with publisher-signed manifests, stores those manifests for lookup, and registers them in an auditable permissioned ledger [2]. Its design recognizes that ordinary distribution transforms artifacts through re-encoding or resizing, requiring provenance certificates to be tracked and reinserted across transformations. In a related domain, provenance templates support structured, repeatable record generation, while service-level tracing and cryptographic evidence make both the resulting record and its construction process non-repudiable [5]. These systems motivate executable tests that evaluate not merely whether records parse or signatures verify, but whether sources, outputs, transformations, and roots remain correctly bound.

The strongest evidence for testing adaptive substitutions comes from work on desynchronized authentication layers. Nemecek et al. show that an artifact can retain a cryptographically valid manifest while conveying provenance semantics that contradict an independently verified watermark [1]. Their “Integrity Clash” arises without cryptographic compromise: a standard editing workflow and omission of an allowed assertion suffice to produce a semantically misleading but independently valid result. Their evaluation uses a conflict matrix covering combinations of provenance and watermark states, realistic perturbation conditions, and classification accuracy across 3,500 images [1]. This demonstrates why substitution tests must distinguish **syntactic or cryptographic acceptance** from **semantic consistency**. An output swap accompanied by recomputed context metadata, paired source-and-output replacement, or complete manifest replacement may evade checks that validate only local hashes or signatures. Separate authenticated-root and unauthenticated-root conditions are therefore essential: a failure beneath an authenticated root tests binding and traversal, whereas an attacker-selected root tests whether the verifier has any external trust anchor at all.

Software supply-chain research provides a broader vocabulary for expected behavior. Okafor et al. identify transparency, validity, and separation as core secure-design properties and analyze attacks across multiple supply-chain stages [4]. Applied to provenance tests, transparency asks whether substitutions remain observable; validity asks whether every asserted source, output, operation, and dependency is authorized and correctly bound; and separation asks whether one actor or stage can rewrite evidence belonging to another. Cross-stage swaps and reordered multi-parent lists are particularly relevant because graph-level meaning may depend on stage identity, edge direction, and parent ordering even when all referenced objects individually exist. Non-malleable coding offers a useful adversarial ideal—tampering should recover the original message or yield an unrelated result rather than a controlled alternative—but it does not itself establish how provenance graph substitutions should be detected [6].

A practical test methodology can borrow from metamorphic testing, which defines relations between an original execution and systematically transformed follow-up executions when a simple oracle is unavailable [9]. Each substitution class can be encoded as a metamorphic relation with an explicit expected result: rejection, acceptance with a changed identity, or acceptance only after re-authentication. Outcomes should record parser success, schema validation, reference resolution, cryptographic verification, root authentication, and semantic graph equivalence separately. This separation matters for schema substitutions, downgrades, and path aliases, since metadata models and serializations must support interoperable interpretation rather than merely preserve bytes [7][8]. The retrieved literature, however, does not establish standard metrics for these exact tests; pass/fail matrices, per-condition detection rates, and false acceptance rates are the most defensible measures suggested by the available work [1][9].

## Gap

No retrieved source systematically executes adaptive substitutions over complete provenance records. In particular, prior work does not compare output swaps with recomputed context metadata, paired source-and-output swaps, source-only and cross-stage swaps, reordered multi-parent lists, schema substitution or downgrade, path aliases, and manifest replacement under both authenticated-root and unauthenticated-root conditions. A new experiment should determine which mutations are rejected cryptographically, rejected only by semantic or graph checks, silently accepted, or accepted after attacker-controlled recomputation—and whether combinations of substitutions expose weaknesses not visible in isolated mutations.
