# Literature survey: Reporting all attempted reference seeds before defining a grokking intervention sample

Grokking experiments are unusually vulnerable to run-selection effects because delayed generalization is not guaranteed to occur under a fixed nominal configuration. The strongest evidence in the retrieved literature comes from Ootani’s multi-seed study, which treats grokking as a rate rather than an outcome observed in one representative run [1]. In an approximately 12K-parameter transformer, grokking depended on dataset coverage and weight decay, while small changes in floating-point execution could change outcomes for individual seeds. Crucially, multi-seed analysis overturned several apparently compelling single-run narratives, including purported task, thread-count, and device effects [1]. This establishes that the reference population for a grokking experiment should be the complete set of attempted seeds under a pinned environment, not only those runs that eventually grok or yield an interpretable checkpoint.

The intervention literature makes this reporting issue consequential. Yıldırım tests architectural hypotheses prospectively by modifying representational magnitude and attention routing before training [2]. On modular addition and multiplication, bounded spherical topology or uniform attention can eliminate the separable memorization phase, whereas the same geometric constraint fails on a non-commutative task [2]. Other work likewise investigates interventions on output temperature and norm [3], or embedding dynamics, sampling, and learning rates [4]. Such studies commonly require comparing trajectories, onset times, or states associated with different training phases. If a main intervention sample is defined only after inspecting whether reference runs grok or contain an “eligible” checkpoint, the resulting analysis addresses a conditional subpopulation—successful reference trajectories—rather than the original seed distribution. That conditioning may be scientifically appropriate for a checkpoint-level mechanistic comparison, but it should be separated from the feasibility question of how frequently qualifying trajectories arise.

Learning-curve methodology provides a broader framework for this distinction. Learning curves measure performance against resources such as examples or training iterations and support decisions including early stopping and model selection [7]. For grokking, a full trajectory is needed to distinguish permanent non-generalization within the training budget from merely delayed onset. Accordingly, a feasibility report should predefine the training budget, grokking criterion, checkpoint-eligibility rule, and treatment of censored runs that have not crossed the threshold by termination. It should then report, for every attempted reference seed, whether training completed, whether grokking occurred, and whether any checkpoint met the eligibility rule. Useful aggregate metrics include the attempted-seed count, completion rate, grokking rate, eligible-checkpoint rate, and onset-time distribution among qualifying runs. These extend Ootani’s seed-rate methodology [1] while preserving the trajectory-oriented perspective of learning-curve analysis [7].

General validation guidance also favors explicit accounting. DOME recommends structured descriptions of data, optimization, model, and evaluation so readers can assess both performance and limitations [8]. Applied here, that structure implies documenting seed generation, numerical environment, stopping rules, exclusions, failures, and the mapping from attempted runs to the final intervention sample. However, the retrieved sources do not directly study whether publishing a seed-level feasibility ledger changes conclusions, nor do they offer a standard definition of an “eligible checkpoint.” The case for complete reporting is therefore methodological rather than an already validated grokking-specific convention.

## Gap

The open question is whether a feasibility study can prospectively enumerate every attempted reference seed—including non-grokking runs, incomplete or failed runs, and completed runs with no eligible checkpoint—before defining the intervention sample, and whether doing so materially reveals selection induced by checkpoint eligibility. A new experiment should distinguish unconditional feasibility outcomes across all attempted seeds from conditional intervention effects among eligible runs. It should also pre-register eligibility and exclusion rules, pin the numerical environment, and retain non-qualifying runs in the denominator. This would directly test whether intervention conclusions are robust to the fragile, seed-dependent reference process documented in prior work [1].
