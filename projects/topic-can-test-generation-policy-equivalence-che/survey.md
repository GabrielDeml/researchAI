# Literature survey: Separating test generation, policy-equivalence checking, and canonicalization for independent validation of canonical archives

The retrieved literature does not directly study canonical archive formats or prove that every canonical archive can be validated by an independently implemented parser and verifier. It does, however, support a decomposition of the problem into distinct concerns. Paschke treats policy verification, validation, and integrity testing behaviorally: tests examine conclusions produced by a policy rather than depending on the policy’s internal structure or rule language [1]. This suggests that policy-equivalence checking can be specified independently of serialization and canonicalization. Two policies—or an original and canonicalized archive—could be compared through their observable decisions on generated cases. The limitation is that test-based agreement is generally evidence of equivalence, not a proof over all possible inputs, unless the policy domain is finite or the tests are derived from a complete semantic characterization.

Parser independence requires an explicit account of the accepted language. Schröder and Cito emphasize that every parser induces a language, even when its grammar is undocumented, and argue that grammars support test-input generation, program comprehension, and language-theoretic reasoning [2]. Their example also shows why independently written parsers may disagree: seemingly simple library operations admit unexpected whitespace, signs, separators, or formatting variants. For canonical archives, the format grammar therefore needs to define byte- or character-level acceptance separately from semantic policy interpretation. Grammar inference could help characterize an existing implementation, but an inferred grammar would document that implementation’s behavior rather than establish the intended archive specification [2]. Jia, Kumar, and Tan provide stronger evidence that parser correctness can be treated as its own verification problem: their parser generator handles ambiguous visibly pushdown grammars, produces parse forests, and formally verifies its core algorithm [3]. This supports using a restricted, explicit grammar and a separately justified parser, although their results do not establish equivalence between two independently developed parsers.

Test generation should likewise remain a separable experimental component. Grammar-based generation can produce syntactically valid archives and boundary cases [2], while differential, metamorphic, mutation, and combinatorial testing are established families for finding discrepancies among implementations or transformations [6]. Applied here, differential tests would compare the reference and independent parsers; metamorphic tests would apply semantics-preserving variations before canonicalization; mutation tests would inject malformed lengths, ordering violations, duplicate fields, or altered policy clauses; and combinatorial tests would cover interactions among archive features. Paschke’s behavioral test-driven approach provides a complementary oracle for policy outcomes [1]. The experiment should report acceptance disagreements, parse-tree or normalized-model disagreements, canonical byte disagreements, and policy-decision disagreements rather than collapsing all failures into one metric.

Formal contracts and reproducibility practices can strengthen this decomposition. Efremov, Mandrykin, and Khoroshilov show how preconditions and postconditions extracted from implementations can form a verification benchmark, while also demonstrating that proof success may depend on specification constructs and memory models [4]. For the archive setting, contracts should separately state parsing safety and completeness, canonicalizer determinism and idempotence, and preservation of policy meaning. Lahiri cautions that mechanical checking against a formal specification does not ensure that the specification correctly captures informal user intent [5]; thus, a verifier may be internally sound yet validate the wrong notion of policy equivalence. Finally, reproducibility requires publishing archives, generators, seeds, implementations, expected outcomes, and alternative analyses, consistent with the concerns described by Krijthe and Loog [7].

## Gap

The untested question is whether these separations are sufficient in combination: can tests generated without reference to canonicalizer internals, a policy-equivalence oracle defined independently of serialization, and a separately implemented parser/verifier jointly validate **every** output admitted as canonical? Existing work supports behavioral policy testing, grammar-based input generation, parser verification, contracts, and reproducible evaluation, but provides no completeness result for canonical archives. A new experiment should therefore distinguish empirical cross-implementation agreement from universal validation, and specifically test whether independently developed components converge on acceptance, canonical bytes, normalized policy structure, and policy behavior across both generated and adversarial archives.
