# Literature survey: Does a larger evaluation budget reveal a success-rate difference?

Evaluation budget is a central basis for comparing genetic algorithms because algorithmic variants may organize reproduction and replacement differently, making generations or cycles incomparable. Jenkins et al. compare generational, steady-state, steady-generational, and \((\mu+\mu)\) genetic algorithms using the number of function evaluations rather than iteration count [1]. Their study targets solving the Schaffer F6 function in fewer than 4,000 evaluations over 30 runs and reports average evaluations alongside ANOVA and Student’s *t*-tests [1]. It also illustrates why operator and replacement details must be controlled: the variants differ in the number of offspring evaluated per cycle, population overlap, elitism, and whether offspring replace the worst, a random non-best individual, or compete with parents [1]. Consequently, an observed success-rate difference at a fixed budget could reflect the algorithms’ differing search trajectories rather than a generic advantage per generation.

The retrieved literature also gives reasons to expect algorithm comparisons to depend on the evaluation horizon. Evolutionary computation is vulnerable to stagnation, diversity loss, population-initialization effects, and premature convergence [2]. These mechanisms can generate nonuniform performance over time: an algorithm may initially progress quickly but later stagnate, whereas another may preserve useful diversity and realize gains only under a larger budget. Work modifying fitness through modeled social interactions is explicitly motivated by avoiding rapid convergence and entrapment in local optima [5]. Likewise, fuzzy genetic algorithms have adjusted control parameters using both best-individual fitness and population-diversity measurements [8]. Although these sources do not directly test success-rate differences across 100,000–500,000 evaluations, they support treating budget as a substantive experimental factor rather than merely allowing longer runs.

For the proposed paired experiment, shared initialization is especially important because population initialization is itself identified as a challenge affecting evolutionary search [2]. Pairing the competing methods on the same initial populations should reduce between-pair variability and isolate differences arising after initialization. Operator-schedule controls are similarly necessary. Jenkins et al. show that crossover choice and replacement strategy are integral parts of GA behavior, comparing single-point, midpoint, and blend crossover within several GA forms [1], while Kadri et al. demonstrate that dynamically adjusted control parameters can alter optimization behavior [8]. Thus, if schedules differ unintentionally across budgets or methods, increased success could be attributed to additional or differently timed operator applications rather than to the focal algorithmic distinction. At each budget, the primary outcome should be binary success under one fixed, prespecified target criterion, summarized as paired success rates and, critically, the discordant pairs in which only one method succeeds. Evaluation count should remain the resource axis; generation count is unsuitable when methods evaluate different numbers of offspring per cycle [1].

## Gap

The retrieved material is thin on the specific inferential question: none of the provided sources reports a paired comparison of success probabilities at 100,000, 250,000, and 500,000 evaluations, nor do they establish whether a difference absent at a smaller budget emerges later. A new experiment should therefore specify three separate hypotheses in advance—one at each budget—while retaining identical paired initializations, operator schedules, success definitions, and stopping rules. The budgets should not be treated as retrospective extensions chosen after inspecting earlier outcomes. This design would determine whether additional evaluations expose a genuine success-rate difference, while separating that question from initialization noise, operator differences, and the known horizon-dependent risks of stagnation and premature convergence [2].
